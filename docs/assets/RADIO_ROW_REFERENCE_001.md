# Radio Row Demolition
**Reference Document: Historic WTC Assets**

## Evidence
Photographic documentation by Berenice Abbott (1930s) and various press photographers in 1965-1966 captured the final days of Cortlandt and Greenwich Streets. The 13-block area known as "Radio Row" was a dense, low-rise commercial district packed with hundreds of mom-and-pop electronics stores before being leveled by eminent domain.

## Dimensions
*   **Building Heights:** Typically 3 to 5 stories (30 to 60 feet high).
*   **Lot Widths:** Very narrow, dense urban lots, often only 20 to 25 feet wide per building.

## Appearance
By August 1966, the blocks were mid-demolition. 
*   **Ground Floor:** Cluttered storefronts with large glass display windows, recessed entryways, and overhanging fabric awnings. Many windows had massive painted "X" marks or "Going Out of Business" signs.
*   **Upper Floors:** Double-hung sash windows, intricate brickwork, and distinct metal cornices at the roofline. Heavy iron fire escapes clung to the front facades.
*   **Exposed Party Walls:** As neighboring buildings were torn down, the sides of surviving buildings were exposed, revealing rough brick, old chimney flues, and faded painted "ghost signs" from decades past.

## Colors
*   **Masonry:** Faded terracotta red, dark brown, and soot-stained tan brick.
*   **Signage:** High contrast—white painted lettering on glass, bright red/blue painted wood signs ("Cortlandt St. Electronics", "Vacuum Tubes", "Surplus").
*   **Trim:** Dark green, black, or grey window framing and cornices.

## Placement
Forming the ragged perimeter of the site. In August 1966, the central blocks were cleared, but the outer edges (especially along Church Street) still had jagged rows of half-demolished structures framing the view.

## Importance
Radio Row provides the emotional and human context of the site. It visually represents the immense scale of what was destroyed to make way for the sterile, modernist mega-project.

## Implementation Notes
Break away from generic background cubes. Use varied heights. Implement specific 1960s typographic signage on the ground floors. Crucially, show the *process* of demolition: expose a side wall with a faded advertisement, or have a pile of brick rubble spilling out of a partially collapsed ground floor.
